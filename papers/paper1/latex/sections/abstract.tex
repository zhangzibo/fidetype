\begin{abstract}
\noindent\textit{Objective.} Neural text-entry decoders often end in a language model that writes fluent sentences from weak neural evidence. We ask when such a system should send (\emph{commit}) a sentence without confirmation, and whether the wrong-commit rate can be bounded.
\textit{Approach.} A trust layer reads the decoder's per-frame output stream, calibrates its confidence, and commits, offers alternatives or abstains. A Learn-then-Test threshold, never below a speed-tuned threshold, bounds the wrong-commit rate with high probability. The rule was chosen after the random re-splits below; all inference is exploratory. We test it offline on one intracortical speech participant (Brain-to-Text '25, 14 days), calibrating before testing in time. Forearm sEMG and MEG typing serve as format and safety checks.
\textit{Main results.} Always committing sent wrong text for 61\% (our decoder) and 19\% (published sentence, gated by agreement with our decoder) of sentences. Calibrated on earlier data, the rule gave pooled wrong-commit rates of 1.0--2.7\% (target 5\%); no test day was significantly above 5\%. Over 50 random re-splits, 8\% (95\% CI 2--19\%) and 2\% (0.1--11\%) exceeded 5\%. The bound covers the mix of days: one day ran at 10\%. The rule committed 15\% and 39\% of sentences; 21\% and 8\% of commits were wrong. A plain confidence threshold committed 198 of 300 no-information streams; with an evidence guard, none.
\textit{Significance.} A stream-level commit rule kept wrong commits rare across days for one participant. It needs an input-validity check and per-session calibration, and commits few sentences with current decoders.
\textit{Clinical trial registration:} BrainGate2, ClinicalTrials.gov NCT00912041.
\end{abstract}
