\begin{abstract}
\noindent\textit{Objective.} Neural text-entry decoders often end in a language model that writes fluent sentences from weak neural evidence, and they rarely signal when a sentence is wrong. We ask when such a system should commit a sentence without confirmation, and whether the rate of wrong commits can be controlled with a stated guarantee.
\textit{Approach.} A trust layer reads only the decoder's per-frame output stream, calibrates its confidence and commits, offers alternatives or abstains. The commit threshold is set on held-out calibration data by Learn-then-Test (LTT) and is never lower than the threshold tuned for speed (max rule). Offline, we test it on one intracortical speech participant in a copy task (Brain-to-Text '25), on forearm surface electromyography (sEMG) key presses composed into sentences (19 participants) and on MEG typing (19 participants, negative control), over 50 random fit/calibration/test assignments. Outcomes are wrong commits per sentence attempted, the commit rate, the error among commits and the speed of a simulated gaze-typing user (effective words per minute, eWPM).
\textit{Main results.} For the one intracortical participant, always committing sent wrong text for 61\% (our decoder) and 19\% (published decoder) of sentences. The LTT max rule gave mean wrong-commit rates of 3.0\% and 3.1\% (target 5\%); 8\% and 2\% of deployments exceeded 5\% (allowed: 10\%). It committed 15\% and 39\% of sentences, of which 21\% and 8\% were wrong, and was 0.11 and 0.51 eWPM faster than the same policy without commits. Conformal risk control met the target only on average: 34--42\% of single deployments exceeded it. A guaranteed error among commits of at most 10\% was reachable only for the published decoder. On sEMG the layer committed at most 9\% of sentences on average, and on MEG none; on MEG and the two weaker sEMG decoders, gaze typing with word completion was faster. Calibrating on data that had also fitted the pipeline raised wrong commits on our decoder (+0.9 percentage points, $p = 0.003$).
\textit{Significance.} A stream-level commit rule can keep wrong commits rare with a high-probability guarantee at little speed cost, but with current decoders it commits few sentences, and the evidence comes from one implant participant.
\end{abstract}
