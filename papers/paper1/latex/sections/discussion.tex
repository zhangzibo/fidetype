\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{The layer is a safety mechanism, not a speed-up.} Against always committing, the trust layer cut wrong commits by an order of magnitude on intracortical speech. Against always offering, the best policy that never commits, it was 1.6\% and 3.6\% faster, for 59 and 44 wrong commits per 1,000 sentences. Whether it beat always committing depended on unmeasured user costs, and on weaker decoders a gaze keyboard with simple word completion was faster still, so we make no general speed claim. What holds across devices and user models is the safety result: the layer commits only when calibrated evidence supports it, its wrong-commit rate is a stated quantity with a bound, and it costs little or no speed relative to always offering. The user or clinician chooses $\alpha$, and the layer reports the commit rate and error among commits that follow. On sEMG and MEG it reduced to always offering, the correct behaviour for decoders whose top sentence was rarely right; it cannot create information that the decoder lacks.

\paragraph{Marginal vs selective risk.} The controlled quantity bounds how often wrong text reaches the recipient per sentence attempted, not how often committed text is wrong (one commit in four on our intracortical decoder; 95\% of the few CRC commits on MEG). We chose it because it is monotone in the threshold, which CRC needs, and because it is the right budget for harm per attempt. A selective-risk controller, for example LTT conditional on the number of commits with a minimum commit rate, is a natural complement that we did not evaluate. Each guarantee should be stated with its assumptions, its target and the measured silent-error rate (\cref{app:guarantees}).

\paragraph{Data reuse can fake a drift failure.} Our earlier analysis read offline CRC above target on later data as within-day drift; with calibration data that played no other role, the excess disappeared into split-to-split variation. CRC needs calibration items exchangeable with new items \emph{for a fixed score function}; when the same items also fit the temperatures and the policy, their scores look better calibrated than new scores, and the threshold is too permissive. The mistake is easy in BCI pipelines, where data are scarce; papers should state which data fitted each stage and compare any claimed shift with an exchangeable reference.

\paragraph{Strong decoders still need a gate.} The published Brain-to-Text '25 decoder has a WER of 0.043, yet 19\% of its sentences were wrong. Gating its text by agreement with the neural evidence kept 96\% of its speed and reduced wrong commits to 4.4\% (2.5\% with a plain threshold), using only the stream, the published text and a light reference decoder. This is what a vendor-neutral stream format enables: one gate and controller for three device types, with adapters of 148 to 568 lines.

\paragraph{Calibration and adaptation.} A frame temperature applied before an LM search requires re-tuning the LM weight. On sEMG, in-session decoder data gave 71\% higher \ewpm{} than another day's data (8.02 vs 4.68): the layer made both safe, but only adaptation made them fast. A 20-item session-start block is too small for a fixed threshold, so it should seed an online controller.
