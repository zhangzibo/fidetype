\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What the commit decision is worth.} Against always committing, the trust layer looks like a large safety gain at no speed cost. Against always offering, which is what most spellers do, the picture is more modest. On our intracortical decoder, committing bought 1.6\% speed for 59 wrong commits per 1,000 sentences; on the published decoder, 3.6\% for 44 per 1,000. Whether this trade is worth it depends on the user and the context, and it should be a setting, not a default. The layer's main value on these data is that it makes the trade explicit and bounded: the user or clinician chooses $\alpha$, and the layer reports the commit rate and the error among commits that follow from it. On sEMG and MEG, the layer almost never committed, so it reduced to an always-offer policy; on MEG, this was the correct behaviour for a decoder whose top sentence was almost never right. The layer cannot create information that the decoder does not have; it can only stop the LM from presenting a guess as an answer.

\paragraph{Marginal vs selective risk.} The controlled quantity, the probability that an attempted sentence is committed and wrong, bounds how often wrong text reaches the recipient per sentence attempted. It does not bound how often committed text is wrong: one commit in four on our intracortical decoder, and nine in ten under CRC on MEG. A user who sees committed text may care more about the second quantity. We chose the marginal risk because it is monotone in the threshold, which CRC needs, and because it is the right budget for harm per attempt. A controller for the selective risk, for example LTT with binomial tests conditional on the number of commits and a minimum commit rate, is a natural complement. \todo{R1: selective-risk controller results (LTT on error among commits) on B2T '25.}

\paragraph{Strong decoders still need a gate.} The published Brain-to-Text '25 decoder has a WER of 0.043, yet it committed a wrong sentence for 19\% of trials, because one wrong word makes a sentence wrong. Gating its text by agreement with the neural evidence kept 96\% of its speed and reduced wrong commits to 4\%. The gate needed only the stream, the published text and a light reference decoder, not the vendor's weights. This is the use case a vendor-neutral stream format is meant to enable.

\paragraph{Decoder adaptation set the speed.} On sEMG, a decoder trained with half a session of the user's own data gave 71\% higher \ewpm{} than one trained on another day (8.02 vs 4.68). The trust layer made both safe, but only the in-session data made the system fast. A session-start block could serve both decoder adaptation and threshold calibration, but our results show that a threshold calibrated early in a day can be too permissive later the same day, and CRC needs at least 19 labelled items to commit at all. Such a block should therefore seed an online controller rather than fix the threshold.

\paragraph{How to state the guarantees.} Our results suggest precise wording. With CRC: ``if the calibration sentences and later sentences are exchangeable, then, averaged over the choice of the $n$ calibration sentences and over a new sentence, the probability that the new sentence is committed wrongly is at most 0.05''. A single session can exceed 0.05. With LTT: ``under the same assumption, with probability at least 0.9 over the calibration sentences, the long-run wrong-commit rate at the chosen threshold is at most 0.05''. With adaptive online re-calibration: ``for any sequence of sentences, the average observed wrong-commit rate over the first $T$ sentences is at most $0.05 + (1 - \lambda_1 + \eta)/(\eta T)$'', which is 0.064 for $T = 816$ and $\eta = 0.05$. All three statements count only the errors that the system observes, so each should be reported with the measured silent-error rate, and none of them says anything about the error among commits.

\paragraph{What standardisation buys.} One stream format let us apply one calibration, gate and risk controller to three device types with adapters of 148 to 568 lines of code. The format is deliberately small; its value is that a consumer such as the trust layer, or an independent auditor, can be written once.
