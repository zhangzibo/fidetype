## 1. Summary of the paper

The paper proposes NeuralTextStream (NTS), a per-frame log-probability format for neural text-entry decoder output. It also proposes a decoder-agnostic "trust layer" that reads only NTS. The layer calibrates confidence (temperature scaling), filters candidates with a neural-evidence guard, and chooses commit / offer / abstain. It also sets the commit threshold with conformal risk control (CRC) or Learn-then-Test (LTT), adapts the threshold online (ACI-style), and logs decisions in a hash-chained ledger. The layer is tested offline on three public datasets: Brain-to-Text '25 (one intracortical participant, T15), the Toronto forearm sEMG key-press data (sentences composed from isolated presses), and SpanishBCBL MEG typing with the authors' own asynchronous decoder. With a simulated gaze-typing user, the layer lowers the marginal wrong-commit rate from 0.19–1.0 to at most 0.059. On B2T and session-calibrated sEMG, speed does not drop. On MEG, the layer never commits and the system only reaches parity with gaze typing.

## 2. Overall recommendation: **Major revision**

The paper is careful and unusually honest about its limits. It has a strong reproducibility story, and the null-input controls are a real asset for a BCI audience. In its current form, I cannot recommend acceptance, for five reasons:

1. **The main comparison is against a strawman.** The baseline is "always commit the top-1". The natural BCI baseline is "never auto-commit, always offer the n-best" (this is what P300/RSVP spellers and most AAC systems do). The authors' own result files show that this baseline is almost as fast as the trust layer and has zero wrong commits (B2T ours: 11.37 vs 11.55 eWPM; published: 18.11 vs 18.77; MEG: 3.23 vs 3.23). The paper never reports it.
2. **The headline metric hides how unreliable commits are.** The "wrong-commit rate" is a share of all items, not of committed items. At the plug-in gate on B2T, one committed sentence in four is wrong (commit precision 0.746). Under CRC on MEG, about 92 % of commits are wrong (0.0193 / 0.021). The paper presents both as successes.
3. **Two of the three "device types" do not test the commit decision.** On MEG the layer commits nothing. On sEMG it commits 0–4 % of sentences, and these sentences are composed from isolated, metronome-paced presses. The cross-device claim in the title and abstract therefore rests on one intracortical participant.
4. **The user model and baseline do not fit the populations.** The MEG and sEMG decoders read overt motor typing by able-bodied people. The simulated user is a gaze typist with ALS. The gaze baseline also has no language model, but the BCI arm has one, and the cited source (Cai et al. 2024) is itself about LLM-accelerated gaze typing.
5. **The related work misses the closest BCI literature.** It omits P300 dynamic stopping, ErrP-based error correction, rejection in myoelectric pattern recognition, closed-loop decoder adaptation, asynchronous "no-control state" detection, and BCI utility metrics. Without these, the novelty claims in §1 and §2 are overstated.

Most fixes are analysis or writing work on data that already exist. Some need new runs, but no new data collection (see §6).

## 3. MAJOR issues

### M1. The "always commit" baseline is a strawman; add "always offer" and "simple threshold" baselines
- **Where:** Abstract; §1 l.24; §6.2 (`results.tex` l.36–45), Table 4 (`tab:main`); Fig. 1; Fig. 6; Discussion ¶1.
- **Problem:** Every speed and safety claim compares the layer with "always commit top-1". No BCI speller or AAC system would ship that. The M2-13 result files already contain a `never_commit` policy (offer only, never auto-commit): B2T ours wrong-commit 0, eWPM 11.37 (trust 11.55); B2T published 18.11 (trust gate 18.77); MEG 3.23 (trust 3.23) (`experiments/2026-11-11-m2-13-conformal/results/tables.md`, l.7, 43, 79). On sEMG the layer commits 0–4 % of sentences, so it already is "always offer" in practice. The marginal value of the calibrated commit decision is therefore +0.18 eWPM (+1.6 %) on B2T ours and +0.66 (+3.6 %) on the published decoder. The price is 4.4–5.9 % of all sentences committed wrongly. This is the real result, and it is a much smaller one than the paper states. The no-guard ablation (§6.8: 0.036 wrong-commit, 11.51 eWPM) shows that a plain threshold on calibrated p_top1 also beats the full layer on safety.
- **Fix [ANALYSIS]:** Add three rows per device to Table 4 and points to Fig. 1: (a) never commit / always offer top-k; (b) commit if calibrated p_top1 ≥ λ, with no guard; (c) commit if raw (uncalibrated) p_top1 ≥ λ. Rewrite the abstract, Fig. 1 and the Discussion around "trust layer vs always-offer". State the trade-off honestly: a commit saves X seconds per sentence and costs Y wrong commits per 100 sentences.

### M2. Report commit precision, not only the marginal wrong-commit rate; reconsider which risk is controlled
- **Where:** §5.1 (definition), §4.4 (CRC loss), §6.2, §6.5 (MEG CRC paragraph), Table 5, Fig. 7b, Discussion "How to state the guarantees".
- **Problem:** The wrong-commit rate = (wrong commits) / (all items). A user who sees a committed sentence cares about P(wrong | committed). The values the paper does not show: B2T ours plug-in 0.0588 / 0.232 = 25 % of commits wrong (the ledger summary itself reports commit precision 0.746, `experiments/2026-11-11-m2-09-provenance/results.md`); B2T published 0.0441 / 0.473 = 9 %; sEMG cross-session 0.004 / 0.02 ≈ 20 %; MEG CRC 0.0193 / 0.021 ≈ 92 %. The marginal loss rewards a layer that commits rarely, and CRC "spends" its budget on almost-certainly-wrong commits (§6.5 says this, but frames it as neutral). Fig. 7b plots errors over *all* items against coverage. The standard risk–coverage curve (Geifman & El-Yaniv 2017, which the paper cites) plots *selective* risk, errors over committed items.
- **Fix:** [ANALYSIS] Add commit precision (with CI) to Tables 4 and 5, and plot selective risk in Fig. 7b. [WRITING] Define both quantities in §5.1. Explain why the marginal risk is the controlled quantity (it bounds the rate of wrong sentences that reach the recipient per sentence attempted). Say plainly that it does not bound the error rate of what the user sees committed. [NEW EXP, optional, cheap CPU] Add a conditional-risk variant (e.g. LTT on selective risk with a minimum-coverage constraint, or conformal selective classification). Compare the two on B2T.

### M3. The claims exceed the evidence: "three device types", "unchanged", "across MEG"
- **Where:** Title; Abstract ("We apply the same layer, unchanged, to three device types"); §1 l.24; Discussion ¶1 and ¶5; Conclusion.
- **Problem:**
  - MEG: the true sentence is top-1 in 2 of 637 instances and in the 10-best for 1.6 %. The layer commits nothing. The MEG audit is a "closed gate" (the authors say so). MEG therefore gives no evidence about the commit decision. It shows only that the guard and threshold do not open on a useless decoder. A constant "never commit" policy shows the same.
  - sEMG: sentences are composed from isolated presses with oracle timing. The layer commits 2–4 % of them (5 of 200 in the null audit, `trust.json` commit_rate 0.025). The "real test" claim in §6.4 rests on 5 commits.
  - "Unchanged": each device has its own fitted temperatures, policy grid, LM weight and adapter. B2T needed a new `stream_text` field. The published-decoder gate puts the published sentence on top of the n-best with a hand-set +2.0 score bonus and word alignment (`experiments/2026-11-10-b2t25-trust/results.md`, "Policy (q)"). The correct claim is "the same code, re-fitted per device".
- **Fix [WRITING]:** Retitle (see §5, item 1). In the abstract, state that the commit decision was tested non-trivially on one intracortical participant. Present MEG as a negative control ("on a decoder whose top-1 is almost never right, the layer correctly never commits"), not as a third positive device. Replace "unchanged" with "the same implementation, re-fitted per device". List the per-device fitted parameters and adapter changes in a table.

### M4. The simulated user and the gaze baseline do not fit the populations, and the baseline has no LM
- **Where:** §5.1–5.2, Appendix A (Table 8), §6.2, Limitations 6.
- **Problems:**
  1. **Population mismatch.** The SpanishBCBL and Toronto signals come from able-bodied people who physically type. The "neural input speed" for MEG (152 cpm) is the healthy participants' keyboard speed. A person who can type at 152 cpm does not need a gaze keyboard. The ALS gaze-typist model applies only to B2T T15. The paper must say this in §5.2, not only in Limitations. It should treat MEG/sEMG eWPM as "what if a user with this decoder's error profile ..." rather than as a user benefit.
  2. **The baseline has no LM.** The BCI arm gets a character 6-gram LM, n-best offers and word alternatives. The gaze arm gets a plain 20 cpm keyboard. Cai et al. 2024 (the source of the 20 cpm and the selection times) is about LLM-based abbreviation expansion for gaze typists, which gave large keystroke savings. A fair "no-BCI" baseline is gaze typing *with* the same LM (word completion or sentence candidates from the same 6-gram). This could erase the MEG parity and shrink the sEMG gains.
  3. **The user model does not encode the motivating phenomenon.** §1 argues that "a fluent wrong sentence is harder to notice than a garbled one". In `aac_gaze()` (`partial_correction=True`), a silent error happens when any wrong word is missed, independently with p = 0.05 per wrong word (`simulated_user.py` l.73–75, 202–208, 365). A sentence with *more* wrong words is therefore *more* likely to give a silent error. Fluency plays no role. This explains why MEG always-commit has the highest silent-error rate (0.235), which is the opposite of the paper's motivation.
  4. **Appendix Table 8 lists a parameter that is not used.** "Probability of noticing a wrong commit 0.9" (`p_detect_error`) applies only to the sentence model. `aac_gaze()` uses the word model.
  5. Only 2 of about 10 parameters come from measurements, and from one user (LP1). The sensitivity analysis (G6) exists only for MEG, and it already flips the MEG conclusion (40 cpm fallback: gaze 6.31 vs trust 5.15; slow selection: 3.15 vs 3.00, G1 `results.md`).
- **Fix:** [WRITING] Move the population caveat into §5.2. Remove `p_detect_error` from Table 8 or explain it. Describe the silent-error model exactly. [NEW EXP, CPU simulation only] (a) Add an LM-assisted gaze baseline (same 6-gram, word completion with k = 3 slots). (b) Run the G6 sensitivity grid on all three devices and report the policy ranking, not only eWPM. (c) Add a variant where the miss probability depends on fluency or LM score, to test the motivating claim. Show which conclusions survive across the grid (a small "robustness matrix" figure would carry more weight than Fig. 6).

### M5. Related work misses the closest BCI literature; novelty claims are overstated
- **Where:** §1 ¶3 ("We know of no layer that ... decides between committing, offering alternatives and abstaining"); §2 (all paragraphs); `refs.bib` (50 entries, none on spellers, ErrPs or adaptation).
- **Missing areas, each directly relevant:**
  - **Dynamic stopping and confidence-thresholded selection in P300/RSVP spellers.** These systems commit a character when the posterior passes a threshold, with LM priors: e.g. Schreuder et al. 2013; Throckmorton et al. 2013; Mainsah et al. 2015 (with ALS users); Speier et al. 2016 (LM-integrated spellers review); Orhan et al. 2012 / Oken et al. 2014 (RSVP Keyboard). This *is* a commit/abstain rule with an accuracy target.
  - **Error-related potentials (ErrPs) for automatic error detection and correction:** Schalk et al. 2000; Ferrez & Millán 2008; Dal Seno et al. 2010; Spüler et al. 2012 (online ErrP correction in a P300 speller); Chavarriaga, Sobolewski & Millán 2014 (review). ErrPs are the BCI field's answer to "silent errors" and to "feedback without explicit correction". They bear directly on the online re-calibration section and on Limitation 7.
  - **Rejection options in myoelectric pattern recognition:** Scheme, Hudgins & Englehart 2013 (confidence-based rejection). This is the direct precedent for the sEMG part.
  - **Closed-loop decoder adaptation and self-recalibration in intracortical BCIs:** Orsborn et al. 2014; Jarosiewicz et al. 2015 (self-calibrating BCI for virtual typing, retrospective target inference); Degenhart et al. 2020; Wilson et al. 2023 / Fan et al. 2023 (unsupervised recalibration). These are the BCI precedents for "online re-calibration from user feedback".
  - **Asynchronous BCI / no-control-state detection:** Mason & Birch 2000; Zhang et al. 2008; Aloise et al. 2011 (asynchronous P300). This is the established framing for "null input yields no committed text", including false-positive rates per minute.
  - **BCI utility and practical metrics:** Dal Seno, Matteucci & Mainardi 2010 (utility metric); Townsend et al. 2010 (practical bit rate). eWPM is a close relative of these. The paper should derive or contrast it.
  - **Confirmation strategies in spoken dialogue systems** (accept / explicit confirm / reject from ASR confidence), e.g. Bohus & Rudnicky 2005, and ASR utterance verification. These are commit / offer / abstain under another name.
  - **Wearable sEMG text entry:** CTRL-labs/Meta, "A generic non-invasive neuromotor interface for human–computer interaction" (Nature 2025; handwriting about 20.9 WPM).
  - **Confirmation in the B2T studies themselves:** Card et al. 2024 had the participant confirm decoded sentences. Say how the trust layer relates to that interaction.
- **Fix [WRITING]:** Add a "Error handling in BCI spellers" paragraph and an "Adaptive decoders" paragraph. Narrow the novelty claim to what is new: a *decoder-agnostic, finite-sample-controlled* commit rule on *sentence-level LM output*, evaluated with stream-level null controls. Resolve the G10 TODOs (Huang et al. 2025; Damri & Laufer-Goldshtein 2026) before submission. The risk-controlled generative ASR paper may be the closest prior work of all.

### M6. Disclose the decoder–stream mismatches and report the decoder metrics a BCI reader expects
- **Where:** §6.1, Table 3, Fig. 5, §5.3, Appendix B.
- **Problems:**
  1. Table 3 gives MEG beam + LM CER 0.475. The trust harness decodes the same streams with other beam settings (frame T 3.30, `lm_weight` 0.5), and its top-1 CER is **0.522** (`experiments/2026-11-11-g1-async-meg-trust/results.md`, "Commit-policy top-1 CER in the harness: async 0.522"). Every MEG trust number comes from the 0.522 pipeline. The paper never says so.
  2. sEMG key accuracy (0.299 / 0.168 / 0.209) is below the majority-class rate (space, 0.338). The text calls the permuted-label 0.022 "the chance level", but uniform chance is 0.037 and majority chance 0.338 (`experiments/2026-11-11-emg-toronto/results.md`). Report balanced accuracy (0.367 / 0.240 / 0.226) and letter accuracy (0.375 / 0.249 / 0.228), and state both chance levels.
  3. MEG WER (0.842 greedy, 0.809 beam) and sentence-exact rate are missing. They explain the MEG trust result far better than CER. Also give the text-only / known-length floor (about 0.69 CER, M1-15 analysis). Without it, readers judge 0.475 against 1.0.
  4. For context, the MEG decoder (0.475 CER) is far behind the published synchronous Brain2Qwerty (0.29) and the v2 EnglishBCBL decoders (15 % word-perfect sentences for the median subject). The paper's MEG result is a statement about one weak decoder on 17 unique test sentences, not about MEG.
  5. The MEG split is within-subject (the same 19 people in train and test; sentences held out). State this in §5.3. It matters for the meaning of the "participant" bootstrap.
- **Fix [WRITING + ANALYSIS]:** Add the harness CER (0.522) to Table 3 and explain the beam settings. Add WER, sentence-exact, truth-in-n-best and the text-only floor per device. Report sEMG balanced accuracy and chance levels. Add one sentence of context on SOTA MEG.

### M7. Selective reporting in the calibration and ablation results
- **Where:** §6.3 title ("Confidence predicts errors, and calibration helps"), §6.8 Ablations, §6.2 sEMG paragraph ("The cross-subject condition stayed below gaze typing").
- **Problems:**
  - On sEMG cross-subject, the *uncalibrated* trust layer reaches 4.76 eWPM [4.60, 4.94] with wrong-commit 0.005. This is above gaze typing (4.27). The calibrated layer gives 4.08, below gaze (`emg-toronto/results/tables.md`, xsubject `d_trust_no_calib`). The cause is known: frame calibration flattens the streams, the LM weight was tuned on raw streams, and CER rises from 0.369 to 0.479. The paper reports the ablation only for session-calibrated sEMG. So the conclusion "cross-subject stays below gaze" is an artefact of the pipeline order.
  - On B2T, frame calibration makes token-level ECE worse (0.090 → 0.130). Session-calibrated sEMG sentence ECE barely moves (0.154 → 0.148). The guard *hurts* on B2T (removing it lowers wrong commits from 0.059 to 0.036 at the same speed), and on sEMG its AUROC is 0.50–0.65. The paper says "the guard did not help on this device", but the guard stays a headline component in Fig. 2, Fig. 3 and §4.2.
- **Fix:** [WRITING] Retitle §6.3 to "Confidence predicts errors; calibration fixes sentence-level ECE but can hurt decoding". Report all ablations on all conditions (the G5 table) and keep the cross-subject result. [NEW EXP, CPU, cheap] Re-tune `lm_weight` after frame calibration (or decode with raw streams and calibrate only the scores) on sEMG and MEG, and re-run the harness. Either justify the guard with a condition where it helps (MEG null inputs: shuffled/zeros/uniform AUROC 0.92–1.00), or make it optional.

### M8. Leakage and independence checks are incomplete for the non-MEG datasets
- **Where:** §5.3, Limitations 11, Appendix B.
- **Problems:**
  1. **sEMG:** test sentences are held-out Tatoeba sentences. The LM is a 6-gram trained on other Tatoeba sentences. Tatoeba is heavily templated ("I know Tom is ..."; examples in the result file include "yanni is young" and "i know tom is charming"). Exact overlap is excluded, but near-duplicates are not checked, unlike for MEG. This likely inflates truth-in-n-best (0.64 session-calibrated), which drives the sEMG offer gains.
  2. **B2T:** 64 of 1,628 cue sentences occur verbatim in the Tatoeba LM text, and one sentence occurs in both fit and test (`b2t25-trust/results/tables.md` header). Near-duplicates are not checked.
  3. **sEMG independence:** "a press can appear in several sentences (only about 10 presses per letter per pool)" (`emg-toronto/results.md`, Limitations). The 551 test sentences share press windows, so sentence-level bootstrap CIs are too narrow. Participant CIs exist for 2 of 3 conditions (the cross-subject participant CI {3.83, 4.36} exists in the tables but is not in Table 4).
  4. **sEMG session-calibrated cross-fitting:** fit sentences come from first-recording presses predicted by `calib_b`, and test sentences from later presses predicted by `calib_a`. Temperatures and policy are thus fitted on a different model's outputs than the test outputs. State this, and check that it does not bias calibration.
- **Fix:** [NEW EXP, CPU] Rebuild the LMs without the 64 B2T cue sentences and without Tatoeba near-variants of the sEMG test sentences (CER ≤ 0.25 rule, as for MEG). Re-run both harnesses (G9). [ANALYSIS] Use participant (or participant × press-pool) clusters as the primary sEMG CI unit. [WRITING] Document the cross-fitting.

### M9. Calibration-set size, exchangeability and "this session" wording of the guarantees
- **Where:** §6.5, Table 5, Discussion "How to state the guarantees", Fig. 9.
- **Problems:** The CRC experiments draw random calibration/test splits from the pooled B2T test half (14 days). Exchangeability holds by construction, and calibration and test mix days. The Discussion then phrases the guarantee as "for this user in this session, given n calibration sentences". That setting was not tested. Its closest proxy, the one-shot "calibrate on earlier, test on later" setting, gives 0.0625 (above α). n_cal = 408 sentences is hours of copy-task data per session. At n = 100, CRC p95 is already 0.094 (B2T ours) and LTT commits only 9.7 % of items. A clinical session gives perhaps 20–50 labelled sentences.
- **Fix:** [ANALYSIS] From the existing per-item tables (`items-b2t-*.json`), add n_cal ∈ {20, 30, 50} (CRC is feasible from n = 19) and splits *within a day* (calibrate on the first k trials of a day, test on the rest of that day). [WRITING] Rephrase the guarantee statements to match what was tested. Move the "Two CRC numbers on sEMG" paragraph into the methods, so that the reader knows from the start which threshold rule each table uses.

### M11. Single seed, single split, few clusters
- **Where:** Limitations 2 and 10; §5.5; Table 3; Fig. 5 TODO.
- **Problem:** Every decoder has one seed. Every B2T and sEMG trust run uses one fit/test split. MEG has 17 unique test sentences (11 validation sentences fit the temperatures and the policy). The MEG decoder CER intervals are over instances, which the authors themselves call "too narrow". The B2T CIs cover trials and days of one person. The paper hedges this correctly, but a JNE reviewer will expect at least seed variance on the decoders and repeated fit/test splits on the trust layer.
- **Fix [NEW EXP]:** 3–5 decoder seeds for the MEG and sEMG decoders (GPU). Repeated fit/test splits (e.g. 10 random day-stratified splits) for the B2T and sEMG trust runs (CPU). Sentence-cluster CIs for MEG CER (G3; [ANALYSIS]). If feasible, k-fold over sentence hashes for MEG (G8), so that all 129 unique sentences (101 train / 11 val / 17 test in the current split) become test sentences once.
